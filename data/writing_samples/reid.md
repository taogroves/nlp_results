The best example of this appeared several weeks into the project. Marcus, Yash, and I had a laser focus on what we wanted to do going into this semester: create a software-based system for a client who did not have the resources or technical knowledge to do it themselves. We found a perfect client right away with the Blue Trunk foundation, who had previously been forced to let go of their web development team due to budget constraints and were forced into hours of tedious copying and pasting just to give their users access to basic information. We knew exactly what to improve, who it would help, and how to get started, which allowed us to skip the “what do we want to make?” phase of design entirely. However, as we began to brainstorm the specific elements of our system, I became concerned that the process was getting out of hand. Because we never had to think about the big picture, we never considered the scope of our work, and what would be realistic to create in just one semester, and I feared we would bite off more than we could chew. Sure enough, our initial design was unrealistic to say the least. We wanted to create a multifaceted, multipurpose database and search system with user accounts to save preferences, a custom admin dashboard showing site analytics and allowing for easy data modification, as well as a custom form for businesses to submit their information AND a custom search algorithm to show the most relevant results (filterable by location of course). The insight I gleaned from this mistake, which did not come until after we realized it and started cutting down on the scope of our project, was how extraordinarily hard it is to be self-aware about the faults in your own thinking. I predicted this would happen, I even warned the team about it, yet I was totally unaware of it as it happened. I was even a huge part of the problem; I had such a grand vision in my head that I pushed for many of the extra bits we didn’t need.
